02 — Ethos

I've never been very good at planning five years ahead.

I usually just follow the work that teaches me the most.

More often than not, that means finding people who know far more than I do.

Every meaningful opportunity began with something I didn't know how to do yet.

I've simply tried to stay curious enough to say yes.

On luck & choice

There's a card game I keep coming back to called Balatro. Every run starts with a hidden seed — a number that quietly determines the cards you'll see, the shops you'll encounter, and the opportunities you'll have. You don't discover what that seed was until the run is over.

At first I thought that was just a clever game mechanic. The more I played, the more it felt like a philosophy.

None of us choose the hand we're dealt. Some people begin with better circumstances, more opportunities, or simply better luck. That's real. But once the run begins, the interesting part isn't the seed — it's the decisions. Which risks you take. What you build around. What you discard. How you adapt when the game refuses to cooperate.

That's how I try to approach life. I can't change where I started, but I can choose what to learn, what to build, and where to spend my energy. Every project, every mistake, every small improvement is another move in the run.

I didn't read that in a book. I looked at the game and saw my own life staring back.

03 — Arc

How the direction got set

  1. /01 The opportunity

    I joined the firm on the technology support desk. It wasn't where I expected to stay, but it gave me a front-row seat to a fast-moving organisation that prioritizes engineering and plenty of opportunities to learn from people who were far more experienced than I was.

  2. /02 The project

    My manager was building a Linux platform from the ground up and invited me to contribute alongside my day job. I took every opportunity I could—during lunch breaks, after hours, whenever there was something new to learn. I wasn't thinking about where it might lead. I simply wanted to understand how experienced engineers approached hard problems.

  3. /03 The lesson

    That project eventually became the firm's production Linux platform, and I moved onto the team full time. Today I lead its engineering and operations. Looking back, I don't think my career has been defined by a master plan. It's been shaped by curiosity. What stayed with me was the mindset: the best opportunities rarely arrive looking like promotions. They arrive looking like difficult problems, generous mentors, and the chance to learn something you couldn't yesterday — and I just kept following the work that taught me the most.

04 — Worlds

Where I spend my time

  • The platform from the story above, in its everyday form: virtualized Linux workstations on VMware vSphere and RHEL. Built from the ground up, it now powers the daily work of some of the firm's most demanding users. It's also become the proving ground for new tooling and AI capabilities—where new ideas are rolled out first, with reliability and user experience guiding every decision.

  • My home lab is where ideas become habits. A Proxmox cluster runs everything from network-wide ad blocking and secure remote access to monitoring, storage, and automation. Some projects are practical — like keeping my family's network a little safer without them ever having to think about it. Others are simply fun, like the Balatro-themed dashboard that greets me every morning Every service exists because it solves a real problem for me or my family. If I won't use it every day, I probably won't build it.

  • My favourite hobbies all have one thing in common: they get me away from a screen. I love travelling to experience new places and cultures, hiking trails, playing badminton, going to the gym, and flying a drone over the wetlands. They all give me a different perspective — and that's exactly where I recharge before diving back into the next project.
